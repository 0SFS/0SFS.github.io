# Creating an Aircraft Model

A repeatable process for building a low-poly aircraft that reads unmistakably as
the real thing and runs cheaply in the browser. It is written from the build of
the Cessna 172; `planes/Cessna_172/` is the worked example to copy from.

The goal is not the lowest triangle count. It is the **most recognisable
aircraft per triangle** — correct silhouette and proportion first, then cut
everything that does not serve them.

## What you need

- Blender 4.1 or newer, runnable headless (`blender -b`). The whole process is
  scripted; nothing is modelled by hand.
- Published dimensions for the aircraft.
- A factory three-view drawing. This matters more than anything else below.

## Why it is scripted

The airframe is generated by a Python script from a table of cross-section
stations rather than sculpted interactively. That buys three things:

- A dimension change is an edit to one number, not a remodel.
- Every level of detail comes from the same source, so they cannot drift apart.
- The work is reviewable — a reader can see *why* a station is where it is.

Set up the workspace before starting:

```text
planes/<Aircraft>/
├── tests/            reference models you were given (inputs, never modified)
└── agent_workspace/
    ├── scripts/      generate_<name>.py, validate_<name>.py, render_<name>_views.py
    ├── measurements/ dimension notes, reference drawings, validation JSON
    ├── renders/      canonical views
    ├── work/         intermediate .blend files (gitignored)
    └── exports/      GLB output and the master .blend
```

## Stage 1 — Measure before modelling

**Do not start from memory or from an existing 3D model.** Correct overall
dimensions do not imply a correct shape; the first attempt at the Cessna 172 was
dimensionally perfect and still looked like a grey banana, because everything
between the endpoints was guessed.

Collect published figures first: length, wingspan, height, wing area, root and
tip chord, dihedral, tailplane span, gear track, wheelbase, propeller diameter,
cabin width and height.

Then derive what the figures imply. Wing area rarely matches a simple root-to-tip
taper — on the Cessna 172 it forced a constant-chord inboard section, which is
visible in the planform and would have been missed by eyeballing.

### Extract stations from the drawing numerically

Reading a scanned drawing by eye is where accuracy is lost. Measure it instead:

1. Find the scale from two known dimensions in different views. On the 172 the
   side view gave 859 px for 8.204 m and the plan view 1157 px for 11.02 m, both
   agreeing on 104.7 px/m — that agreement is the check that the scale is right.
2. Check the attitude. Factory drawings are often posed. The 172G is drawn with
   the nose gear compressed, 1.35° nose-low, measurable from the offset between
   the nose and main wheel contact points. Every vertical reading needs
   correcting for it.
3. Sample the drawing per column and per row. `scripts/col_query.py` reports the
   ink runs in any image column or row, which turns "roughly here" into a number.
4. Write every value into `measurements/` with its source, and record the
   derivations, not just the results.

### The measurement that matters most

Get the **longitudinal split** right before anything else: how much length sits
ahead of the wing leading edge versus behind it. The first Cessna 172 blockout
had 2.75 m ahead and 5.53 m behind; measurement gave **1.94 m and 6.34 m**. That
single error — 0.8 m too much nose, 0.8 m too little tail — was what made it read
as a stubby generic high-wing. Fixing it did more than every other change
combined.

## Stage 2 — Blockout, then look at it

Build only the major masses: fuselage, wing, tailplane, fin, gear, wheels,
propeller, struts. No detail.

Render ten fixed orthographic views — front, rear, left, right, top, bottom and
the four 3/4s — into a contact sheet, and **actually look at them**. Use fixed
camera framing rather than fit-to-object, so successive iterations can be
compared directly and a change cannot hide behind a reframe.

If the blockout does not read as the aircraft, stop and fix the masses. Adding
detail to wrong proportions never rescues them.

### Build the fuselage as a loft, never a stretched primitive

Define explicit cross-section stations and loft through them. Each station
carries its longitudinal position, floor and roof height, the height of maximum
width, half-width, and a super-ellipse exponent for the upper and lower halves so
a section can be round, flat-topped or flat-bottomed as the real one is.

This is what a stretched capsule cannot do. The Cessna 172's section runs **round
cowl → flat-topped cabin under the wing → round tail cone**, and the flat top is
not cosmetic: the wing carry-through sits on it and the wing's lower surface *is*
the cabin roof.

### Shape notes that carried the Cessna 172

Each of these was wrong in an early pass and visibly cost recognition:

- **The tail-cone top line is nearly level**; almost all the taper comes from the
  belly sweeping up. Converging both lines symmetrically gives a generic dart.
- **The cabin roof follows the wing's lower surface** across the wing chord, so
  the two are one structure with no gap and no poke-through at the thin trailing
  edge.
- **The wing trailing edge is straight**; the taper is taken on the leading edge.
- **The horizontal stabiliser sits just below the tail-cone top line**, not at
  mid-height.
- **The fin needs its dorsal fillet** and a forward-raked rudder hinge, so the
  rudder is wider at the base.

The equivalents for a different airframe will differ, but the method is the
same: find the two or three lines that carry the silhouette and get those right
before spending a triangle anywhere else.

## Stage 3 — Iterate against the drawing

Loop: change geometry → re-render the ten views → find the largest remaining
mismatch → change geometry. Keep every iteration's renders; being able to compare
back is what catches a regression.

Compare against the drawing directly. Stacking your side view under the factory
side view at matched scale exposes errors that look fine in isolation.

Fix in priority order, and do not polish anything below the first item that is
still wrong:

1. Silhouette and overall proportion
2. Fuselage side profile and cross-section
3. Cabin, windshield, greenhouse
4. Nose and cowling
5. Wing planform and dihedral
6. Tail proportions
7. Landing gear
8. Propeller and spinner

---

## Stage 4 — Spend triangles where they earn recognition

Rules that came out of this build and are worth applying to any airframe.

**Nothing renders that cannot be seen.** Hidden geometry costs every frame. The
Cessna 172's glazing was first built as duplicated faces offset above the
fuselage: 40 duplicated triangles *and* 40 fuselage triangles permanently
occluded beneath them, so the GPU drew 80 to show 40.

**Reach for a material index before new geometry.** Windows, cabin posts and
rounded glazing corners are all a second material on faces that already exist.
Same draw-call count, no extra vertices, nothing hidden.

**Assign per triangle when a whole face is too coarse.** A quad is already two
triangles, so painting one of them gives a diagonal edge for free — that is how
the windshield and rear-window corners are chamfered.

**Choose cross-section ring angles deliberately.** Evenly spaced vertices put
nothing at the window sill or head, which is what forced the glazing into a
separate shell in the first place. Placing vertices where features actually fall
let the highest level drop from 12-point to 10-point rings with no visible loss.

**Open the fuselage where another part closes it.** The roof is omitted across
the wing chord because the wing seals it — except at the trailing edge, where the
wing thins to a knife edge and stops covering the hole.

**Watch for techniques with hidden constraints.** Putting a raked band in the
face diagonals locks its width to its rake offset, so a thin band can only come
out near-vertical. The rear cabin post needed an explicit cut to make width and
rake independent — which also removed a station and ended up cheaper.

**Quote the real saving.** One of these optimisations was worth two triangles.
Say so rather than dressing it up.

## Stage 5 — Name the parts and place their pivots

The runtime finds moving parts **by node name**, so these names are an interface:

```text
Fuselage      Wing_Left / Wing_Right       HorizontalTail_Left / _Right
VerticalTail  Rudder                       Elevator
Aileron_Left / Aileron_Right               Flap_Left / Flap_Right
LandingGear_Nose / _Left / _Right          Wheel_Nose / _Left / _Right
Spinner       Propeller                    Strut_Left / Strut_Right
```

Every animated part needs its **origin on its hinge line** — the aileron and flap
on their span-wise hinges, the elevator on the hinge at the centreline, the
rudder on its raked hinge, the propeller on the hub. Get this wrong and the
surface swings instead of hinging.

Export with **identity rotation on every node**. The animation code assumes a
part's local axes are the glTF axes; a baked rotation silently breaks the sign
of every deflection.

Verify after exporting rather than trusting it:

```bash
blender -b --factory-startup \
  --python planes/<Aircraft>/agent_workspace/scripts/verify_rig.py -- path/to/model.glb
```

Confirm all seven moving parts exist, each has an identity rotation, and each
pivot sits at the expected station.

## Stage 6 — Levels of detail

Four levels. Drop the cheapest-to-lose thing first, in this order: cross-section
resolution → control-surface separation → part separation.

| Level | Target | What it keeps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LOD0 | 1000–1500 tris | Everything, separately addressable |
| LOD1 | 400–700 | Same names, coarser sections |
| LOD2 | 150–300 | Control surfaces merged into their panels |
| LOD3 | ≤100 | Static parts joined into one mesh; propeller separate |

Generate every level from the same parameterised script — never decimate. A
decimator does not know that the lift struts matter more than the cowl's
roundness.

**Every level keeps the features that identify the aircraft.** For the Cessna 172
that is the high wing with dihedral, both lift struts, the tricycle gear, the
swept fin, the dark greenhouse and the two-blade propeller. LOD3 came in at 130
against a target of 100, and that was the right call: the only remaining cuts
were the struts, gear and glazing, which are exactly what separates it from a
generic monoplane at any distance.

Coarse levels legitimately lack the control-surface nodes. The runtime binds
whatever it finds, so this needs no special handling.

## Stage 7 — Validate

Run a validation pass that reports, as machine-readable output:

- Overall length, wingspan, height against the published figures
- Triangle, vertex, object, mesh, material and texture counts
- Bounding box, and that the wheels sit on `z = 0` so the aircraft can be placed
  on a runway without a fudge factor
- Degenerate triangles, loose vertices, unapplied transforms
- Open boundary edges, per object
- Left/right symmetry error

Two of these will flag things that are correct on purpose. Say which and why:

- **Open boundaries** are expected wherever a surface is deliberately not sealed
  — the fuselage roof under the wing, uncapped wing root ribs buried inside the
  body.
- **Symmetry error** should come only from genuinely asymmetric parts. On the
  Cessna 172 the sole source is the propeller, whose blades are twisted.

Anything else in those two lists is a real defect.

---

## Stage 8 — Export and wire in

Author in Blender with the **nose along +Y and up along +Z**, which is Blender's
default glTF export orientation. Put the model origin **on the ground between the
wheels**, laterally centred, longitudinally at the centre of gravity.

Export one GLB per level into `public/aircraft/<aircraft-id>/`.

Then, in `src/flight/aircraft/aircraftCatalog.ts`, add an entry with the levels,
their triangle counts, and the chase distance at which each takes over. Two
values usually need attention:

- `modelYawRad` — glTF points the nose along -Z, the simulator along +Z, so this
  is normally `Math.PI`. It must stay a rotation; mirroring would silently
  reverse the propeller's twist.
- `modelOffset.y` — the simulator holds the aircraft reference point
  `aircraftClearanceMeters()` above the terrain, 1.65 m at level attitude, while
  the model's origin is on the ground. Adjust if the parked stance looks wrong.

`docs/aircraft-assets.md` covers the runtime side in full.

An airframe with an empty `lods` array is still selectable and falls back to the
block placeholder, which is a reasonable way to land the entry before the mesh
exists.

## Finishing check

Look at all ten views again and ask plainly: would someone familiar with the type
identify this immediately as *this aircraft*, rather than merely a small plane of
roughly the right layout?

If not, the answer is more shape, not more detail.

Then be honest in writing about what is still wrong. The Cessna 172's report
records that its cowl is slightly conical in plan, that the glazing band is
shorter than the drawing's, that there are no panel lines, antennas or wheel
fairings, and that the gear track follows the modern figure while the shape was
measured from an older drawing. None of that stopped it being usable, and a
reader who knows the limits can decide what to fix next.
